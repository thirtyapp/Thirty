# THIRTY Motion Language

**Version:** v1.0.1
**Status:** Approved v1.0.1

## Purpose

Define how THIRTY moves.

Movement is part of the product language. It is never decoration.

## 1. Motion Philosophy

Movement in THIRTY is never added because animation is possible. Every instance of motion must be traceable to one of five purposes:

- **It supports intention.** Motion should follow something the user meant to do, not happen to them. It confirms a choice already made rather than manufacturing one.
- **It reinforces ritual.** The daily Circle is a ritual, not a task list (Playbook Ch.1 §4). Motion at ritual moments — opening, closing, returning — should feel like part of the ritual's shape, not an effect layered on top of it.
- **It reduces cognitive load.** Movement can show a user where something came from and where it is going, so they do not have to reconstruct that relationship themselves (Playbook Ch.2 §4). Motion that requires the user to think about the motion itself has already failed at its one job.
- **It provides spatial continuity.** A transition's job is to make a change feel expected and safe, connecting one state to the next without a jarring cut (Playbook Ch.2 §4, World System §14).
- **It communicates meaningful change.** Motion is one of the ways THIRTY tells the user that something real happened — a Circle filled, a session began, a day closed — never a way of making a screen feel more alive for its own sake.

Movement must never exist simply because animation is possible. An animation that cannot be explained by one of the five purposes above is decoration, and decoration does not move on its own (Playbook Ch.2 §4).

> **Formal principle: Motion exists to support intention.**

## 2. Design Principles

- **Movement is calm.** A movement's job is to lower the user's sense of urgency, never to raise it (Playbook Ch.2 §4). A transition that makes a screen feel more urgent than the action that triggered it is wrong, not the action.
- **Movement is purposeful.** Every motion must be traceable to one of the five purposes in §1. If it cannot be explained, it should not exist.
- **Movement is continuous.** Motion connects one state to the next; it does not interrupt the user's sense of where they are (World System §14).
- **Movement never competes with content.** The Circle, and whatever the user came to see or do, always outrank the motion around them (Playbook Ch.3 §2).
- **Movement slows down.** Speed communicates weight. Something significant is allowed to take a breath; nothing in THIRTY needs to resolve instantly to feel responsive (Playbook Ch.2 §4).
- **Movement is subtle.** THIRTY's motion is felt more than it is watched. A movement that draws attention to its own craftsmanship has drawn attention away from what it was meant to support.
- **Movement respects stillness.** Nothing moves without the user, or without a reason tied to their action. Ambient or idle motion that exists only to make a screen feel dynamic has no place here (Playbook Ch.2 §4).
- **Motion rewards attention.** A user who watches closely should notice that a movement was considered — but nothing about THIRTY should require that attention to be understood.
- **Motion respects focus.** At any moment, motion supports the one thing the user is doing; it does not introduce a second thing worth looking at (Playbook Ch.2 §7, Ch.3 §2).
- **Motion should disappear.** The best motion in THIRTY is the motion a user never consciously registers, because it made the natural next thing feel inevitable rather than performed.

> **Formal principle: Nothing in THIRTY should ever feel in a hurry.**

## 3. Motion Categories

### A. Ritual Motion

**Examples:** First Breath, Circle Opening, Circle Closed.

**Purpose:** Support the daily ritual.

Ritual Motion accompanies the small number of moments that carry the Circle's meaning directly — the reveal of a new day (World System §13, First Breath), a Circle beginning, and above all a Circle closing (Playbook Ch.1 §12, Brand Book §10). These moments earn the least spectacle in the entire product, not the most (Playbook Ch.2 §4). Restraint here is not an oversight; it is the clearest, most tested application of the Golden Rule in the whole product.

### B. Interface Motion

**Examples:** buttons, cards, navigation, state transitions.

**Purpose:** Provide clarity.

Interface Motion is the ordinary motion of using the product — a button responding to a touch, a card appearing, a screen changing. Its entire job is legibility: showing the user what just happened and what is available next. It should be quick and quiet, never a moment in its own right (§6, Immediate/Quick).

### C. World Motion

**Examples:** mist, birds, clouds, grass, water, light.

**Purpose:** Bring the World to life.

World Motion is the restrained, ambient movement inside an illustrated World — the setting that accompanies a recommendation, a session, and a completion (World System §5, §8). It is atmosphere, not spectacle, and it always remains subordinate to the Circle (see §9 below).

### D. Growth Motion

**Examples:** new flowers, fuller trees, evolving landscape.

**Purpose:** Reflect long-term personal growth.

Growth Motion marks the permanent, cumulative changes a World undergoes as a user returns over time (World System §11, Personal Growth). It is slow, permanent, and never announced — the user simply notices, at some point, that something has changed. It carries no streak logic and never regresses after an absence.

## 4. Motion Hierarchy

Motion should always have a clear visual hierarchy. This is a separate axis from the Motion Categories in §3 — a Category describes *what kind* of motion something is (Ritual, Interface, World, Growth); the Hierarchy below describes *how much attention* a given instance of motion is allowed to claim at any one moment. Every animation belongs to one of three levels.

### Primary Motion

**Purpose:** The movement that deserves the user's full attention.

**Examples:** The First Breath wordmark, Circle Opening, Circle Closed.

Only one Primary Motion may exist at a time. Each of these three moments is its own Primary Motion, in its own sequential moment — never simultaneous with another (§5, Motion Budget).

### Secondary Motion

**Purpose:** Support the Primary Motion.

**Examples:** illustration fade, heading fade, recommendation reveal, button appearance.

Secondary Motion must never compete with the Primary Motion.

### Ambient Motion

**Purpose:** Create atmosphere.

**Examples:** drifting mist, birds, grass, clouds, subtle light movement.

Ambient Motion should almost disappear into the background. It exists to create life rather than attract attention.

> **Formal rule:**
> "Primary Motion leads.
> Secondary Motion supports.
> Ambient Motion breathes."

## 5. Motion Budget

Every screen has a limited motion budget. Motion should always support the primary focus of that screen — the same discipline that already governs a single primary action per screen (Playbook Ch.2 §7) and a single visual hierarchy (Playbook Ch.3 §2) applies equally to movement.

If one element is performing an important motion, surrounding elements should remain quiet. Two significant movements competing for attention at once are not twice as engaging — they are a sign that one of them does not belong on that screen right now (Playbook Ch.4 §4, The Calm Filter).

> **Formal rule: One Primary Motion at a time.**

## 6. Timing

THIRTY does not define motion timing in milliseconds. The correct duration is not a number — it is a feeling: does this movement feel rushed, or does it feel considered (Playbook Ch.2 §4). Where the feeling and a stopwatch would disagree, the feeling wins.

Instead, motion is reasoned about in emotional timing categories:

- **Immediate.** A response that must feel like a direct extension of the user's touch — no perceptible delay between action and reaction.
- **Quick.** A small, everyday interface change — confirming a tap, revealing a small piece of information. Fast enough to feel efficient, never fast enough to feel abrupt.
- **Natural.** The default pace for most transitions — the speed of an unhurried, deliberate gesture. Most of THIRTY's motion lives here.
- **Slow.** Reserved for moments carrying more weight than an ordinary transition — a meaningful change the user should have time to register, not rush past.
- **Ceremonial.** Reserved for Ritual Motion (§3.A). These moments are allowed to take longer than their content strictly requires, because the pause itself is part of what makes them feel significant.

Ritual moments belong to the Ceremonial category. A Circle closing, or a new day beginning, should never be timed the same way as a button press.

## 7. Easing

**Preferred:**

- ease-out;
- ease-in-out;
- gentle deceleration.

**Avoid:**

- bounce;
- spring;
- elastic;
- overshoot;
- playful exaggeration.

Movement should always feel soft and intentional — the natural, unhurried easing already named in Brand Book §16, never the mechanical or the playful. A movement that overshoots its destination and settles back asks to be noticed; THIRTY's motion is not meant to be noticed, it is meant to be felt (§2, "Movement should disappear").

## 8. Choreography

Motion tells one story at a time.

> One thought.
> ↓
> One movement.
> ↓
> Pause.
> ↓
> Next thought.

Multiple important elements should never compete for attention simultaneously (§5, Motion Budget). A pause between movements is not dead time — it is what lets the previous movement finish meaning something before the next one begins. Choreography without a pause reads as noise, however well each individual movement was designed.

## 9. World Motion

Movement inside a World should emerge from the World itself, not be added on top of it (World System §5, §8). It is restrained, atmospheric, and always secondary to the Circle.

**Walking**

- birds;
- grass;
- drifting clouds.

**Meditation**

- mist;
- water;
- reeds.

**Reading**

- curtains;
- warm light;
- plant leaves.

**Stretching**

- light;
- curtains;
- subtle air movement.

Of these, only **Walking** (Quiet Trail) is fully specified as an approved reference World today — its motion vocabulary (birds, subtle atmospheric drift) is drawn directly from World System §15. Meditation, Reading, and Stretching are named only as placeholders in World System §16, with no approved World DNA yet; the motion vocabulary above is offered here as the illustrative extension of the same reasoning, consistent with each category's Emotional Intent (World System, Emotional Intent table), not as a ratified specification. Full World DNA for these categories remains a future decision — see §16 and the unresolved questions in the accompanying summary.

World Motion should always remain subordinate to the Circle.

## 10. Haptics

Haptics are rare. A haptic is a physical promise, and a product that spends it often teaches the body to ignore it (Playbook Ch.2 §5). THIRTY currently reserves haptics for exactly two ritual moments:

**Start Circle**

Purpose: Commitment.

**Circle Closed**

Purpose: Completion.

Routine haptics must never be introduced elsewhere — not for marketing, milestones, re-engagement, or any other interface event. The First Breath (World System §13) intentionally remains silent: it is a ritual moment, but not one of the two moments haptics are reserved for.

## 11. Accessibility

Reduced motion settings must be respected. When a user has asked for less movement, THIRTY should reduce complexity and reduce duration — never remove the meaning a movement was carrying (Playbook Ch.3 §13).

Movement should remain understandable even when simplified. A simplified transition that still shows the user where something came from and where it went has succeeded; one that merely appears and disappears without that relationship has lost the one thing motion was there to provide (§1).

## 12. Premium Motion

Premium deepens motion. It never changes the language.

**Examples:**

- richer Ambient Motion;
- smoother transitions;
- additional environmental detail;
- more nuanced light.

This follows directly from the standing Premium rule (Playbook Ch.1 §9, Ch.2 §9, Ch.4 §5; World System §12): a Premium World breathes more, it does not become a different World. Premium must never introduce flashy or distracting animation, and the free experience must never feel incomplete by comparison — the same test already applied to every other Premium concept applies here without exception.

> **Formal rule: Premium enriches motion. Premium never changes its philosophy.**

## 13. Silence

Stillness is part of the experience.

Not every screen needs movement. Not every interaction deserves animation. Ambient or idle motion that exists only to make a screen feel alive has no place in THIRTY (Playbook Ch.2 §4) — stillness is not an unfinished state waiting for a designer to add something to it; it is frequently the correct, finished one (Playbook Ch.3 §5, Ch.4 §4).

Sometimes the most meaningful design decision is to let the interface remain completely still. A screen that says or shows nothing is not a gap — it is the visual equivalent of the silence already described for sound (Playbook Ch.2 §6) and for whitespace (Playbook Ch.3 §5).

> **Formal principle: Stillness is part of motion.**

## 14. Signature Motion

THIRTY may eventually define one or more signature motions — a movement distinctive enough to be recognised as THIRTY's own, the way a specific gesture or transition can become inseparable from a product's identity.

This is intentionally left undefined in Motion Language v1.0.

A signature motion must emerge naturally from product evolution rather than be invented prematurely. Manufacturing one now, ahead of the product experience that would give it meaning, would be exactly the kind of motion this document already warns against — movement added because it is possible, not because it is earned (§1).

This section exists only to reserve the concept.

## 15. Review Checklist

Before any future animation ships, it should be able to answer these honestly:

- Does this animation have a purpose?
- Does it support the Circle?
- Does it improve clarity?
- Does it reduce cognitive load?
- Does it respect stillness?
- Is it calm?
- Is it subtle?
- Is it the only Primary Motion on screen?
- Would removing it make the experience worse?
- Does it align with THIRTY's Motion Language?

An animation that cannot be answered well on all of these is not ready, regardless of how well it is crafted.

## 16. Document Relationships

- **The [Playbook](../playbook/README.md)** governs philosophy — why THIRTY looks, feels, and behaves the way it does.
- **[DESIGN_SYSTEM.md](../DESIGN_SYSTEM.md)** governs visual components — what a colour, spacing, radius, or type value is.
- **[WORLD_SYSTEM.md](../worlds/WORLD_SYSTEM.md)** governs illustrated environments — what a World is, and how it varies and grows.
- **This document, the Motion Language,** governs movement — when THIRTY moves, how it feels, and what it must never do.
- **Future implementation ADRs** will govern technical implementation — specific durations, easing curves, and the rendering approach used to express what this document defines.

None of those documents is modified by this one. Where a future decision in any of them would require contradicting a claim made here, that contradiction is resolved deliberately and explicitly — never by quietly overriding one document with another.
