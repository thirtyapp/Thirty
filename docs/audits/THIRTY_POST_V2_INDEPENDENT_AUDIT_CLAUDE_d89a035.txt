## BLIND VERDICT
**C — TECHNICALLY STRONG, BUT THE COMMERCIAL DESIGN IS STILL TOO WEAK** (as a recurring subscription).

## EXECUTIVE CONCLUSION

THIRTY V2 is now a real, honest and well-made product. It is no longer a pretty timer:
- Feedback changes what you're offered.
- Activities have natural lengths and visible guidance.
- There is one bounded way to say "not this one".
- Memory is editable and the History screen is humane.
- Nothing claims more than the evidence supports.

On product craft and trust it is better than most consumer wellbeing apps at launch.

The Free problem it solves is real but low-urgency. "What small thing could help today?" is a daily question. But the answers come from a list of 16 live activities that most people could name themselves: a walk, music, tidying, reading, a warm drink, a stretch. THIRTY's value is removing the decision and framing the act calmly. That's worth installing. Whether it's worth opening every day without any pull is unproven, and the design deliberately gives up the usual pulls.

Personalisation is real but mostly invisible. The engine learns properly: bounded, decaying, with rests and exploration. But the only on-screen evidence is one line, "You found this useful before.", plus the Memory page. By Day 30 the user has seen every activity for their main need, and "learning" is a reordering of a short list they already know. It will improve outcomes subtly. It's unlikely to *feel* like THIRTY understands them.

Premium is a large improvement on V1 and is now honest, but structurally it is **orchestrated bundling**:
- Each of the 13 modules is a live Free activity, plus a purpose line and an optional short form.
- A routine is two Free activities run back to back in the Guided shell.
- A 7-Circle Path picks among at most three possible pairs, each full or short.

The process is careful and truthful. The thing it produces is modest, and a user could write it in a note.

Two implementation facts weaken ownership more than the documents admit:
1. **A user cannot run their own routine on demand.** There's no "use it today" anywhere (verified in the notifier API and on the routine page). It only appears when the engine picks it, roughly weekly in the founder's own simulations, and less often than its own pieces in SIM 6.
2. **A routine has a hidden rest you can't lift.** A "Not useful" during the Path trial (for example on Circle 6's shorter form) is attributed to the routine. It rests the routine the user has just chosen to keep for 14 days, and nothing in Memory or the Toolkit says so. SIM 3 shows exactly this: *My reset* was kept on D27, wasn't offered on four ≈30-minute Clearer Head days, and first appeared on D60.

Maintenance is genuine, honest and deterministic, but it fires **rarely** by design. Time misfit is roughly a one-off per routine and window. Fading needs at least 3 rated uses of a routine that appears about weekly. A gap needs Free to be serving the user *badly*, and a user like that has probably churned. The honest stable check then removes the last reason to pay. For a monthly subscription, the rational behaviour is to subscribe, build two or three routines over 4–6 weeks, keep them (they're kept by design) and cancel. That build-and-cancel pattern is the central commercial fact. It comes from the architecture, not from copy or polish.

That isn't a reason to take routines hostage. Keeping them after cancellation is right for trust. It is a reason to doubt monthly-subscription fit, and to treat billing shape (annual, one-time, or Free-first) as the commercial lever. The offer page still says "Billed monthly."

So the product deserves real users now. More desk work won't answer the open questions, which are about demand. There is one concrete defect to fix first (the hidden routine rest), and one founder decision worth taking before the closed test (letting users run their own routine on demand). The verdict is C because, judged from the code, the *paid* design is unlikely to sustain monthly renewal. It is not C because the product is bad.

## CORE PROBLEM
**Strength: MODERATE as a free daily aid, WEAK as a paid problem.**
- It's frequent.
- It's mildly valuable on hard or foggy days, where "just tell me one thing" really helps.
- It's barely painful on ordinary days.
- People already solve it adequately (walk, tea, tidy).
- Decision fatigue here is real but shallow. The cost of a wrong pick is ten minutes.
- Software help is wanted mostly by people who already like rituals and calm apps. That's a real but narrow segment.

## FREE

- **Installing:** yes. It's beautiful, free, has no account and is clear within 30 seconds.
- **Keeping:** plausible, because it costs nothing to keep.
- **Opening regularly:** only for users who adopt it as a ritual. There's no pull beyond a reminder, and the visible return on each open is small.

### Day 1
The flow is: name → "What would help most today?" (three needs with plain meanings) → time → one activity, its authored reason, a how-to sheet and "Not this one?" → a Circle at its natural length (Open moments rotate; Guided has steps) → a calm end → optional reflection.

It's clear, low-friction and credible. The starter order is curated, and the sparse-data honesty costs nothing because nothing personal could be said yet.

**What the user loses if THIRTY disappears after Day 1:** almost nothing. It was a nicely framed suggestion they could have made themselves.

### Day 7
- A "Very useful" activity comes back after at least 4 days, labelled "You found this useful before."
- "Not useful" rests it, and the acknowledgement says so.
- Memory lists Useful before, Resting and Not suggested.
- The improvement is **real but subtle**. Most days still show the activity's authored reason, not a personal one.

**Lost after Day 7:** a short, correct memory of what helped and what didn't, and the effortless pick.

### Day 30
- For their main need, the user has seen all 4–6 options within about two weeks.
- The engine now orders a known list well, and avoids what they disliked.
- A "usual time" appears in Memory.
- Value accumulates, but it isn't *felt* as understanding.

**Risk: "pleasant but easy to forget" is the likely outcome for most users.** That's because the ceiling is the catalogue, not the engine.

## PREMIUM
Verified: Premium does a different *job* from Free. It deliberately builds a small owned object and then watches it. Verified limits:
- **Pieces:** every piece is a Free activity; nothing new happens inside a routine except sequence and a single Circle.
- **Path:** the output space per Path is tiny.
- **Running a routine:** routines can't be started by the user.
- **Maintenance:** triggers are rare in a satisfied user.
- **Lapse:** fair and exact. Starting builds, tunes and shorter versions is gated, routines stay Free candidates, and a Path is saved at its Circle and resumes exactly.
- **Content treadmill:** none, which is good for scalability and bad for "what's new this month".

## USEFUL VS PREMIUM-WORTHY VS SUBSCRIPTION-WORTHY
- **Useful: YES.** A Path is a sensible guided experiment that ends with something named.
- **Premium-worthy: MARGINAL.** A one-time or low annual charge for "build your routines" is defensible. As a headline paid benefit it's thin, because the output is two Free activities in sequence.
- **Subscription-worthy: NO, as monthly.** Recurring work is rare, and the main output is kept after cancellation. Annual is borderline: it works only if users value occasional upkeep plus the option to build again.

## SEVEN-CIRCLE QUESTION
- Seven is **long relative to the decision space**. Circles 1–3 try pieces, Circle 4 pairs them, and Circle 6 tests a shorter form, which is real learning.
- Circle 5 usually repeats Circle 4, and Circle 7 ("as it stands") usually repeats Circle 4 or 5. That's confirmation, bordering on padding.
- A Path only advances on days that match its need, so 7 Circles take about 2.5–4 weeks for a mixed-need user. That eats a large share of a monthly subscriber's first month.
- 5 Circles (try, try, pair, shorter, confirm) would likely produce the same routine most of the time.
- It's TUNABLE. Don't change it now; read it in the closed test (completion and "why am I still on this?" comments).

## MODULE LIBRARY
- There are 13 modules, all live, with good role ordering and honest short forms. Three live activities aren't modules: quiet reading, single-task focus and tend-to-one-small-thing.
- Diversity is limited by the safety gate. Energy has no bodyweight or stairs content.
- Composition adds:
  - one continuous guided Circle;
  - an order (opener, then main, then closer);
  - a truthful shorter form.

  It adds no transitions, no new instruction and no integration. An Open piece becomes one step, "About n minutes".
- **Verdict: closer to BUNDLING than to ORCHESTRATION + LEARNING.** The learning is real but narrow: it chooses among about three pairs and two lengths. Combining two pieces is mostly what you'd get by doing two Free activities back to back.

## TOOLKIT
- **Ownership:** naming, versions, origin, rename/disable/delete, routine-level Don't-suggest and exportability are all real.
- **Bookmark vs routine:** a routine is better than a bookmark because of its history and maintenance. It is *worse* than a bookmark in one way: **you can't open it and do it.**
- **Size:**
  - One routine is a near-empty page (screenshot d13).
  - 2–3 routines make a reasonable Toolkit.
  - The gap cap is 6, but there are only 6 templates, so clutter is unlikely.
- **Versioning:** understandable but managerial ("Version 2", "Earlier versions", "Use version 1 again?").
- **Switching cost:** value-based and fair, but small, because the routine is easy to remember.

**"What makes My reset different from saving two activities in a note?"** Today, three things:
1. It came from your own answers over seven tries.
2. It runs as a timed, guided Circle.
3. THIRTY offers it on days it fits and offers to fix it when it stops fitting.

The note wins on one thing that matters to users: you can use it whenever you want.

## ROUTINE OWNERSHIP
It's real at the data level and weak at the experience level. The user owns a routine they can't invoke, that appears about weekly, and that can be hidden for two weeks by an answer they gave during the trial. Ownership currently feels like "THIRTY keeps a file about my routine" rather than "this is mine to use".

## ROUTINE FREQUENCY
**Classification: LIKELY PRODUCT ISSUE.** The evidence goes beyond the closed-test framing:
- **SIM 6:** the routine is rated "Very useful" every time and still appears 5 times in about 6 weeks. Its own pieces appear alone about as often (Move to music 4×, active task 4×).
- **SIM 3:** zero appearances for about 5 weeks after it was built (the rest defect plus time fit).
- "My screen break" is structurally 30-minute-only.
- **The engine is still right** not to give routines a bonus. The problem is that the *only* way to use a routine is to be picked.

No preference toggle is needed. On-demand use would remove most of the issue without touching ranking integrity.

## MAINTENANCE

### Fading
- **Thresholds:** at least 3 rated uses, and then the last 3 score ≤ 0, or the last 2 score ≤ −1, or a "Not useful" after 2 or more positives.
- Credible and not jumpy, but slow in practice. At about weekly appearances and a realistic answer rate, it takes 1–2 months of evidence.
- A 3-Circle tune-up that swaps one piece is meaningful but small. A user could just as easily swap the piece themselves, if they could edit.

### Time misfit
- This is the **strongest Premium job**. It's visible, truthful (every piece kept), and easy to understand.
- It's roughly a one-off per routine and per window, not recurring work.
- It only exists for routines whose pieces have short forms. "Drink + Gentle stretch" can never be shortened.

### Gap
- Served-well suppression works. SIM 5b shows no offer when Free answers are "Somewhat useful", and a gap needs at least 3 explicit answers.
- It won't manufacture Paths. But it fires mostly for users whom Free is failing, who are the least likely to be paying subscribers.
- Templates already built are never offered again.

### User refresh
- Tune and Shorter are available anytime, which is useful control.
- It isn't enough recurring value on its own: it's a feature you use once or twice a year.

### Stable check
- "Your routines are working well — nothing to change" builds trust and simultaneously says Premium has no current job.
- It needs at least 3 rated routine uses in 28 days. Many users will see "Still learning how these fit." month after month, which is honest but empty.

**Cadence: RARE** in steady state, and occasional only in the first two months.
- **Monthly billing:** poor fit.
- **Annual:** viable only if the build phase plus occasional upkeep feels worth a yearly fee.
- **Pause/resume:** natural, since users come back when life changes. But Google Play monthly billing doesn't monetise that well.

## STABLE-USER PROBLEM
**STRUCTURAL.** The design rightly refuses to manufacture work, degrade routines or hold them hostage. Under those rules, the honest answer to "why keep paying?" is "insurance in case your routines stop fitting".
- **Monthly:** that doesn't renew.
- **Annual:** it renews only if the first build was valuable enough to buy again a year later.
- **Pause/resume:** this is the natural fit, but it only pays if re-entry is easy and priced for occasional use.
- **Voluntary cancellation** will be high and calm. **Reactivation** is plausible, because the Path is saved exactly and offers stay visible with "Tuning routines is Premium".

## LAPSE / CANCELLATION
Verified fair: routines stay owned and offered, the controls stay, Path progress is saved and resumes at the exact Circle, and only starting builds, tunes and shorter versions is gated.

Both effects are real:
- **(A) Trust:** it lowers the risk of trying Premium and protects the brand.
- **(B) Retention:** it removes the main reason to stay subscribed.

**Structurally, B matters more under monthly billing, and A matters more under one-time or annual billing.** The fix is billing shape, never hostage mechanics.

## DIFFERENTIATION
- **CORE:** one daily decision with no browsing; explainable, editable local memory ("What THIRTY remembers").
- **SUPPORTING:** the Worlds and the calm close; natural-length Circles; the explicit time choice; no-pressure, no-streak positioning; lapse fairness.
- **GENERIC GOOD DESIGN:** Not this one; History; export/delete; reminders.
- **NOT DIFFERENTIATING (as experienced):** Paths, user-owned routines and maintenance. They're well executed, but what they produce is what any routine app, coach or chatbot produces.

## CHATGPT / AI SUBSTITUTE TEST
"Give me a 20-minute reset routine for a busy head" and saving the answer in Notes reproduces the **Premium output** well. A chatbot also adapts ("it's raining, I have 10 minutes") with more flexibility than THIRTY.

What THIRTY adds:
- zero typing and zero prompting;
- a remembered, editable evidence trail;
- THIRTY noticing misfit and fading without the user asking;
- a calm in-session runtime;
- privacy and predictability.

Being AI-free is a **trust strength for a niche, irrelevant to most users, and a flexibility weakness** against substitutes. For Free, the low-effort ritual is the defensible part. For Premium, the substitute is good enough for many users.

## CALMNESS VS COMMERCIAL URGENCY
- **For Free and the brand: A**, healthy differentiation at a manageable cost.
- **Combined with a monthly subscription whose recurring work is rare: B**, a structural retention weakness.

Don't fix it with pressure. Fix it with billing that matches episodic value: annual, one-time, or a "build" purchase.

## CONTENT-TREADMILL TEST
**PARTLY.**
- A *new* subscriber gets full value with zero new content, because six Paths are enough to build a Toolkit.
- A *tenured* subscriber gets only rare maintenance.
- The architecture avoids a content treadmill by having little self-renewing work, not by having work that renews itself without content.

## FOUNDER-LABOR SCALABILITY
- **Content, serving, billing:** scale without founder effort.
- **Maintenance logic:** deterministic.
- **QA:** heavy. Every tuning change needs simulation re-reading and device passes, which is founder-time-intensive but doesn't grow with user count.
- **Hidden human dependencies:**
  - **acquisition**, with no organic loop, so it will be founder-driven marketing;
  - **content and safety review**, needed for every activity added.

## TOP 7 FAILURE MODES (probability × impact)
1. **Build-and-cancel.** Users subscribe, build 1–3 routines in 4–6 weeks, keep them and cancel. Rational under monthly billing.
2. **Low-urgency drift.** Free is pleasant but non-essential; opens decay after week 2 with no trigger beyond a reminder.
3. **Acquisition cost beats a low-price subscription.** The category is crowded and THIRTY has no viral or organic engine.
4. **"Two Free activities stuck together."** The Premium offer is understood but not valued, so conversion stays low.
5. **Hollow ownership.** Routines appear rarely, can't be started, and are hidden by an invisible rest, so the Toolkit feels pointless.
6. **Invisible learning.** Personalisation is real but felt as "same short list", so Day-30 users don't feel understood.
7. **Low reflection answer rate.** It starves learning, fading, gap and the stable check together, making both tiers look static.

## PRE-RELEASE BLOCKERS
1. **Routine-level rests are hidden, can't be lifted, and are triggered by Path-trial answers.**
   - A "Not useful" on any Path Circle with the same pieces, such as Circle 6's shorter form, is attributed to the routine. See `pastCirclesFrom` in `recommendation_provider.dart`, which matches on `samePieces`.
   - It rests the routine the user has just kept for 14 days, then down-ranks it for 14 more (`routineDaysSinceNotUseful` in `recommendation_engine.dart`).
   - Memory and the Toolkit never show it, and there's no Suggest again for routines.
   - It's verified in the founder's own SIM 3: kept on D27, absent on four ≈30-minute days, first offered on D60.
   - It confounds the main Premium closed-test question (routine frequency) and breaks the contract's visible, reversible rest rule.
   - Fix it before testing. It's a bounded fix.

Nothing else qualifies as a blocker.

## FOUNDER DECISIONS

**1. Can a user run their own routine on demand?**
- **Option A:** add "Use it today" on the routine page. It replaces today's not-yet-started Circle, once a day, and it's not a catalogue.
- **Option B:** keep engine-only appearances and ask about it in the closed test.
- **Recommendation: A, before the closed test.**
- **Why:** without it, "routines of your own" is partly untrue in experience, and the closed test would mostly measure a design constraint you can already see.
- **Commercial consequence:** it raises perceived ownership and Toolkit value. It slightly *increases* build-and-cancel risk, which is honest, and pushes the billing decision.

**2. Billing shape for validation** (Phase E scope, but decide its framing now).
- **Option A:** monthly-led, as the offer copy says now.
- **Option B:** annual-led, plus a one-time or "lifetime Toolkit" option.
- **Other:** Free-only closed test, asking willingness to pay without charging.
- **Recommendation: B, tested against the monthly option.**
- **Why:** the value is episodic and front-loaded, and the routines are kept.
- **Commercial consequence:** a lower headline monthly ARPU, but less churn damage, and it matches the anti-hostage promise.

There is no other founder decision before Phase E. Path length and thresholds are TUNABLE and should be read from tests.

**IS THERE ANYTHING MATERIAL WE CAN STILL LEARN OR FIX WITHOUT REAL USERS THAT JUSTIFIES DELAYING PRE-RELEASE? YES**, narrowly.

Beyond that, the answer is **no**. Every remaining uncertainty is about demand: return, perceived learning, Path appeal, routine value, willingness to pay and billing shape. More simulations, ADRs or tuning can't resolve those. The previous audit noticed the pattern of judging the product against its own contracts rather than against users. V2 has corrected most of the substance, and the remaining risk is exactly what desk work can't settle.

Blind verdict C stands as a diagnosis: I'd expect the recurring-subscription design to underperform. But the right response is to test with honest billing options and clear kill signals, not to rework the commercial design blind. If the closed test confirms build-and-cancel, the lever is billing shape (annual or one-time), never hostage mechanics or pressure.
